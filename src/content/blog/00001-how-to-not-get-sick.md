Every year, right around the first real temperature drop, someone says it: *"careful, you'll catch a cold in this weather."*

I have heard this my whole life and it is, strictly speaking, wrong. Weather does not give you a cold. Viruses do. You cannot catch a cold from being cold.

And yet — everyone does seem to get sick when the weather changes. So either the saying is nonsense or there is something underneath it worth understanding.

It is the second one.

## Weather doesn't make you sick. It changes the conditions.

The actual explanation is less mystical and more interesting. When the temperature drops, several things happen at once:

- **We move indoors.** Windows close, air recirculates, and you spend hours breathing the same air as thirty other people. Respiratory viruses spread through air first and surfaces second.
- **The air gets dry.** Cold air holds less moisture, and indoor heating strips what is left. Dry air dries out your nasal passages, and those passages are the first line of defense — mucus traps viruses and moves them out. When they dry out, that defense weakens.
- **Our routines fall apart.** Late nights, less movement, worse food, more stress around holidays and deadlines. Sleep and stress are two of the biggest levers you have over your immune system, and both take a hit in the same window.
- **Seasonality is real.** Influenza and many cold viruses genuinely peak in colder months. It is not the temperature — it is the human behavior that comes with it.

So the saying is not completely wrong. It is just pointing at the wrong culprit. The weather is the *signal*, not the *cause*.

## The stuff that actually works

If you want to get sick less often — and let's be honest, "never" is not on the menu — these are the things with actual evidence behind them. I have roughly ordered them by how much they matter.

### 1. Wash your hands like you mean it

Not a quick rinse. Soap, warm water, twenty seconds, especially before eating and after coming home. Most respiratory and stomach bugs enter through your hands and then your face. This single habit does more than everything else on this list combined, and it costs nothing.

Hand sanitizer is a decent substitute when you are out, but it does not work on everything (norovirus, for example, laughs at it). Soap and water remain undefeated.

### 2. Stop touching your face

Easier said than done — people touch their face dozens of times an hour without noticing. But it is the delivery mechanism. Handwashing that ends with you rubbing your eye thirty seconds later is wasted effort.

You will not fully stop. Just building the awareness cuts the frequency meaningfully.

### 3. Sleep is not optional

This one hurts because it is the first thing we sacrifice. But short sleep genuinely impairs immune function, and the effect shows up fast. In controlled studies, people sleeping under six hours are dramatically more likely to catch a cold when exposed to the virus than people sleeping seven-plus.

No supplement on this list comes close to the effect of simply sleeping enough. If you only fix one thing, fix this.

### 4. Ventilate

Open a window for ten minutes a day, even in winter. It feels counterintuitive — "won't I get cold?" — but fresh air dilutes whatever is floating around in your room. If you are in a shared office or classroom, this matters a lot.

### 5. Get the flu shot

It is not perfect. Some years it is 40% effective, some years better. But it is safe, cheap, widely available, and reduces both your chance of getting the flu and the severity if you do. You do not have to be enthusiastic about it. Just get it.

### 6. Stay home when you're sick

Half the reason everyone gets sick in the same two months is that sick people keep showing up to work and class. If you are coughing and sneezing, you are the weather change for someone else.

## The stuff that's mostly myth

I want to be fair to these, because some have a grain of truth. But the grain is small.

| Claim | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| "Cold air gives you a cold" | Viruses give you colds. Cold air may mildly affect your defenses, but it is not the cause. |
| "Vitamin C prevents colds" | No meaningful effect for most people. Might shave a little off duration if you already take it regularly, but it will not stop you getting sick. |
| "Feed a cold, starve a fever" | Neither. Eat normally. Your body needs energy to fight infection, not less of it. |
| "Sweat it out" | Sweating does not remove a virus. It just makes you tired and dehydrated while you are already sick. |
| "Antibiotics will help" | Not against viruses. Colds and flu are viruses. Antibiotics do nothing except upset your gut and breed resistance. |

## The margin: things that help a little

Once the big six are handled, these add a modest edge. None of them replace sleep or handwashing.

- **Exercise regularly.** Moderate activity is associated with fewer and milder infections. Extreme endurance training temporarily suppresses immunity, so the dose matters.
- **Eat real food.** Not a specific superfood — just enough protein, vegetables, and fiber that your body is not running on fumes.
- **Manage stress.** Chronic stress measurably suppresses immune response. This is the same advice as "sleep more," just harder to follow.
- **Get outside.** Sunlight and movement, especially in winter, help mood and vitamin D. If you are genuinely deficient in vitamin D (worth testing, common in northern winters), supplementing is reasonable. If you are not deficient, it probably will not do much.

## A simple checklist

If you want the whole post compressed into something you can actually do tomorrow:

1. Wash your hands when you get home and before you eat.
2. Sleep seven to eight hours.
3. Open a window for ten minutes.
4. Get the flu shot this season.
5. If you get sick, stay home and do not be a hero about it.

That is it. No powders, no protocols, no twelve-step morning routine. Just the boring stuff that actually works.

You will still get sick sometimes. Everyone does. The goal is not zero — it is fewer, shorter, and milder. That is a much more achievable target, and it is the one worth aiming at.
